<p>The smell of cocoa drifting through camp smoke is enough to make any kid abandon a s'more. But when dairy is off the table, the usual campfire treats can feel like a minefield. That's why I started making these dairy-free kids brownies in foil packets—they're rich, fudgy, and simple enough that even a tired parent can pull them off after a long day of hiking.</p>

<h2>Why Foil Packets Work at Camp</h2>
<p>Foil packets are the unsung heroes of campfire cooking. They contain the mess, cook evenly, and let kids help without getting too close to the flames. The key is using heavy-duty foil—regular foil will tear when you flip the packet. I double-layer mine for extra security, especially if we're cooking over a bed of coals that's hotter than expected.</p>
<p>Another trick: place the packet on a small grate or a flat rock near the fire, not directly in the flames. You want gentle, even heat—think of it like a low oven. If the fire is roaring, wait until it dies down to coals. This isn't just about even baking; it's also safer. A flare-up can singe the packet and ruin your dessert.</p>

<h2>Ingredients That Travel Well</h2>
<p>One of the best things about this recipe is that most ingredients are pantry staples. Coconut oil works beautifully because it's solid at room temperature—no need for a cooler that's already crammed with ice packs. But if you're in a warm climate, you might want to keep it in a shaded spot. Unsweetened oat milk is my go-to for camping because it doesn't need refrigeration until opened, and it adds a subtle creaminess.</p>
<p>For the chocolate chips, look for ones labeled dairy-free—many brands are, but check the label. If you're camping with kids who are sensitive to cross-contamination, read carefully. I've found that a handful of chips makes the brownies extra gooey, which kids love. But if you're watching sugar, you can skip them.</p>

<h2>Step-by-Step: Making the Batter at Camp</h2>
<p>Start by mixing the dry ingredients at home. Put the flour, cocoa, baking powder, and salt in a labeled zip-top bag. When you're ready to bake, just dump the bag into a bowl and add the wet ingredients. This saves time and reduces the chance of a wind gust blowing flour everywhere.</p>
<p>At the campsite, melt the coconut oil in a small pot over low heat or just let it soften in the sun. Whisk it with the milk, sugar, and vanilla. Then stir in the dry mix until just combined—don't overmix, or the brownies will be tough. If you're using a bowl, make sure it's stable; a wobbly camp table is a recipe for disaster. I often set the bowl on the ground between my feet.</p>

<h2>Cooking Over the Fire: The Right Heat</h2>
<p>Here's where most people go wrong: they place the packet directly over flames. The outside burns while the inside stays raw. Instead, wait for the fire to die down to glowing coals. Rake them into a flat bed and place the packet on top. If you have a grate, use it—it adds a little distance and prevents the foil from puncturing.</p>
<p>Cook for about 15 minutes, then check. The time varies wildly depending on your fire. A toothpick inserted through the foil should come out with a few moist crumbs. If it's still wet, give it another 2 minutes. Don't peek too often—you'll let the heat escape.</p>
<p>When they're done, use tongs to lift the packet off the coals. Let it rest for a couple of minutes before opening. The steam inside is hot, and a curious kid can get burned. I always warn mine to stand back when I unfold the foil.</p>

<h2>Adapting for Wind, Rain, and Picky Eaters</h2>
<p>Windy evening? Set up a windbreak with a cooler or a camp chair. A gust can blow your packet right off the coals. Rain? Move the operation under the tarp, but keep the fire at a safe distance—never cook inside a tent. If the weather turns truly nasty, you can bake these in a Dutch oven on a camp stove instead.</p>
<p>For kids who turn up their noses at anything new, let them add their own mix-ins. Dairy-free chocolate chips, a few raspberries, or a sprinkle of cinnamon. Giving them a choice makes the brownies feel like their creation. And if you're camping with a child who has a dairy allergy, double-check every label—cocoa powder and baking powder are usually safe, but flavored varieties might contain milk.</p>

<h2>Cleanup and Storage</h2>
<p>The best part? No dishes. After the brownies are gone, let the foil cool completely, then fold it up and toss it in the trash. If you're in bear country, store any leftover brownies in a sealed container in your bear bag or vehicle—sweet smells attract wildlife. I've learned that the hard way, and it's not a lesson you want to repeat.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>Dairy-free kids brownies are more than a treat—they're a way to make every camper feel included. With a little prep at home and a patient fire, you can serve up warm, fudgy squares that rival any bakery. So next time you're packing the camp box, toss in a bag of cocoa and a bottle of coconut oil. The smiles are guaranteed.</p>
