<p>The smell of a campfire is good, but the smell of pizza dough hitting a hot cast iron skillet is better. Especially when you thought you'd have to give up pizza for the whole trip because you're gluten-free and dairy-free. You don't. I've tested this method on a two-burner camp stove and over a bed of coals, and it works. The trick is to treat the crust like a flatbread, not a yeast-risen dough. You get a crispy bottom, a tender middle, and a pizza that actually holds together when you pick it up.</p><h2>Why GFCF Pizza Works at Camp</h2><p>Most gluten-free pizza doughs are sticky, fragile, and need a hot oven. At a campsite, you don't have a pizza stone or a 500°F oven. But a cast iron skillet is the great equalizer. It holds heat evenly, sears the bottom of the crust, and can go from stove to fire to table. The dough I use is a simple blend of gluten-free flour, baking powder, and water—no yeast, no rising time, no proofing. It's more like a thick flatbread, which is exactly what you want when you're cooking over an open flame.</p><p>The dairy-free part is easier than you think. Skip the pre-shredded cheese (it's coated with anti-caking agents that don't melt well) and shred a block of vegan mozzarella at home. Keep it in a zip-top bag in the cooler. By the time you're ready to cook, it's cold but not frozen—perfect for melting.</p><h2>The Dough: Make It at Home or at Camp</h2><p>You can mix the dough at home and bring it in a sealed container, or make it fresh at the campsite. If you're making it at home, roll it out between two sheets of parchment paper, stack the rounds, and refrigerate. At camp, just peel off the top paper and slide the dough into the skillet. If you're mixing at camp, use a bowl and a spatula—no mixer needed. The dough will be sticky, but that's okay. Let it rest for 10 minutes after mixing. This is the secret. The flours need time to absorb the water, which makes the dough less tacky and easier to shape.</p><p>One mistake I see people make is adding more flour to stop the stickiness. That makes the crust dry and crumbly. Instead, dust your hands and the parchment with rice flour. Rice flour doesn't absorb moisture the way wheat flour does, so the dough slides right off.</p><h2>Cook It Right: Skillet, Stove, or Fire</h2><p>For a camp stove, preheat a 10-inch cast iron skillet over medium heat for a few minutes. Add a little olive oil—enough to coat the bottom. Place the dough in the cold? No, wait until the oil shimmers. Then lay the dough in. Cook for 3–4 minutes until the bottom is golden and the edges look set. Flip it with a spatula. Now you have a cooked side up. Spread the sauce, add the cheese and toppings, then cover the skillet with a lid or a piece of foil. This traps the heat and melts the cheese without burning the bottom. Cook for 5–7 minutes, checking once or twice.</p><p>If you're using a campfire, wait until the flames die down to glowing coals. Place a grill grate over the coals and set the skillet on top. The heat is more intense than a stove, so you'll need to rotate the skillet occasionally to avoid hot spots. I've also used a Dutch oven—put the pizza inside, place the lid on, and set the whole thing on the coals. That works beautifully, but it takes a few minutes longer.</p><h3>Wind and Rain: The Campsite Variables</h3><p>Wind is the enemy of a camp stove. It can blow out the flame or cause uneven heating. Set up a windbreak—a large cutting board or a piece of cardboard propped against a rock works. If it's raining, cook under the vestibule of your tent, but never inside the tent itself. Carbon monoxide from a stove is deadly in an enclosed space. Keep the stove at least a few feet from the tent entrance, and never leave it unattended.</p><h2>Toppings That Travel Well</h2><p>Choose toppings that don't need refrigeration or that hold up in a cooler. Pepperoni, olives, roasted red peppers, and caramelized onions are all great. Fresh basil is a nice touch, but it wilts quickly. Instead, pack a small jar of dried oregano or a squeeze bottle of basil pesto (make sure it's dairy-free). Mushrooms are fine, but sauté them first so they don't release water on the pizza and make the crust soggy.</p><p>If you're camping with kids, let them pick their own toppings. It's a fun activity and they're more likely to eat what they make. Just keep the cheese and sauce in separate containers so you can assemble quickly.</p><h2>Cleanup and Leftovers</h2><p>Cast iron is easy to clean at camp. Scrape off any stuck bits with a spatula, then wipe it out with a paper towel. If there's stubborn residue, add a little water and bring it to a simmer, then scrape again. Dry it over the stove, then rub a thin layer of oil over the surface. That's it.</p><p>Leftover pizza? Wrap it in foil and reheat it in the skillet over low heat the next morning. It's a great breakfast with a cup of camp coffee.</p><h2>The Bottom Line</h2><p>GFCF pizza at camp isn't just possible—it's a highlight. The key is a simple dough, a hot skillet, and a lid to melt the cheese. Once you've got that down, you'll never go back to cold sandwiches on pizza night. So next time you're packing the cooler, add a bag of flour and a block of dairy-free cheese. Your taste buds will thank you.</p>
