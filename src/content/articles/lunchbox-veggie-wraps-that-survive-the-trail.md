<p>The first time I made veggie wraps for a day hike, I used a flimsy tortilla and loaded it with juicy tomatoes. By lunch, the whole thing was a soggy mess. Since then, I've learned a few tricks that keep wraps crisp and intact—even after hours in a backpack. Here's how to build lunchbox veggie wraps that actually survive the trail.</p>

<h2>Start with the Right Tortilla</h2>
<p>Not all tortillas are created equal. For camping, skip thin corn tortillas—they crack and fall apart. Instead, look for sturdy flour or whole-wheat tortillas that are pliable. If you're going on a multi-day trip, check the label for shelf-stable options that don't need refrigeration. They're a lifesaver when you're away from a cooler.</p>

<h2>The Moisture Barrier Trick</h2>
<p>The biggest enemy of a veggie wrap is sogginess. The solution is a spread that acts as a moisture barrier between the tortilla and the wet vegetables. Hummus is my go-to—it's flavorful and thick. Cream cheese or a bean spread also work well. Spread it generously, leaving a one-inch border so the wrap seals properly.</p>

<h2>Choose the Right Veggies</h2>
<p>Crunchy vegetables hold up best. Shredded carrots, cucumber slices, bell pepper strips, and spinach are all great choices. Avoid watery veggies like tomatoes or pickles unless you're adding them right before eating. If you do want tomatoes, pack them separately and stuff them in at lunchtime.</p>

<h2>Rolling Technique That Keeps Everything In</h2>
<p>Rolling a wrap tightly is a skill. Start by folding the bottom edge over the filling, then fold in the sides, and roll away from you, tucking the filling in as you go. The tighter the roll, the less likely it is to burst open. If you're making these with kids, let them practice on a few—it's a fun way to get them involved.</p>

<h2>Wrapping for the Trail</h2>
<p>Once rolled, wrap each wrap in parchment paper or foil, twisting the ends to seal. This keeps the wrap from unrolling and also helps it stay fresh. I like to label each one with a marker so everyone knows which is theirs. Then pack them in an insulated lunch bag with an ice pack, especially on warm days. If you're hiking without a cooler, plan to eat the wraps within a couple of hours.</p>

<h2>Adapting for Weather and Terrain</h2>
<p>On a hot day, the ice pack is non-negotiable. If you're hiking in cold weather, the wraps will stay cool naturally, but you might want to add a thermos of hot soup to pair with them. For a long, rocky trail, pack the wraps in a hard-sided container to protect them from getting squashed.</p>

<h2>Kid-Friendly Variations</h2>
<p>Kids often prefer simple flavors. Stick with hummus, shredded carrots, and mild cheese. Let them build their own wraps at the campsite—it's a fun activity and they're more likely to eat what they make. You can also cut the wraps into pinwheels for smaller hands.</p>

<h2>Food Safety on the Trail</h2>
<p>Remember that hummus and fresh veggies need to stay cool. If you're out for more than a couple of hours, use an insulated bag with a cold pack. When you're done, pack out all food scraps—leave no trace. And always wash your hands or use hand sanitizer before handling food, even at the campsite.</p>

<p>With these tips, your lunchbox veggie wraps will be the highlight of the trail. They're easy to make, pack well, and taste great at the summit.</p>
