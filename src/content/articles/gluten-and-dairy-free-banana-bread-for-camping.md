<p>The first time I packed a loaf of banana bread for a camping trip, it was a sad, crumbly mess by the time we reached the trailhead. Store-bought gluten free breads often turn to sawdust in a backpack. But a homemade loaf, baked with care and wrapped in parchment, can be the best thing you eat all weekend. This gluten and dairy free banana bread is moist, sturdy, and forgiving—exactly what you want when you're cooking over a fire or just need a quick breakfast before the hike.</p>

<h2>Why This Recipe Works at Camp</h2>
<p>Banana bread is a camper's best friend. It packs flat, needs no refrigeration, and tastes better on day two. This version uses simple swaps: a 1:1 gluten free flour blend and any dairy free milk you like. The result is a loaf that holds together without being dense. The key is using very ripe bananas—the spotty ones you'd otherwise toss. They add sweetness and moisture, so you can cut back on sugar.</p>

<p>One trick I've learned: measure your flour by spooning it into the cup and leveling it off. Gluten free flours are lighter than wheat flour, and scooping directly can pack in too much, making the bread dry. That little habit has saved more loaves than I can count.</p>

<h2>Baking at Home vs. at Camp</h2>
<p>If you're short on time, bake this loaf at home and bring it along. Cool it completely, then wrap in parchment paper and foil. It'll stay fresh for two days in a cool spot. For longer trips, slice it and freeze—it thaws in a few hours in your cooler.</p>

<p>But baking it at camp? That's a whole different joy. A Dutch oven placed in the coals of a campfire gives the bread a slightly smoky edge that no home oven can replicate. You'll need a 10-inch Dutch oven and a chimney starter to get coals going. Aim for about 8 briquettes underneath and 14 on top. That puts you around 350°F. Bake for 45-55 minutes, rotating the oven a quarter turn every 15 minutes so the heat stays even. Check with a skewer—if it comes out clean, it's done.</p>

<p>If you're using a camp stove instead, a folding camp oven works well. Place it on a low flame and rotate the pan halfway through. Just watch it closely; camp stoves run hot.</p>

<h2>Adapting for Weather and Altitude</h2>
<p>Camping brings variables. At high altitude (above 5,000 feet), baked goods rise faster and can collapse. Add an extra tablespoon of flour and reduce the baking powder or soda by a quarter. At sea level, follow the recipe as written.</p>

<p>Humidity is another factor. If the air is thick with moisture, you may need to bake a few minutes longer. If it's dry and windy, cover the Dutch oven lid with a towel to keep ash out, and check the bread earlier than the minimum time.</p>

<h2>Make It Your Own</h2>
<p>This base recipe is a blank canvas. Add a handful of dairy free chocolate chips, chopped walnuts, or dried cranberries. For a tropical twist, fold in shredded coconut. Just keep the add-ins to about 1/2 cup so the loaf doesn't get too heavy.</p>

<p>For a more portable option, bake the batter into muffins. Reduce the baking time to 18-22 minutes. They're easier to grab on the trail and fit perfectly in a small container.</p>

<h2>Safety Notes for Camp Baking</h2>
<p>When using a Dutch oven, always set it on a stable, heatproof surface away from tent walls and flammable gear. Wear leather gloves when handling the hot lid. And never cook inside a tent—carbon monoxide from any flame is a silent danger. Let the coals cool completely before leaving camp, and scatter them when they're cold to the touch.</p>

<p>For food storage, keep the wrapped loaf in a sealed container or bear canister if you're in bear country. Sweet smells attract wildlife, so don't leave it out on the table overnight.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>This gluten and dairy free banana bread is more than a recipe—it's a camping staple. Whether you bake it before you leave or over a fire, it delivers comfort and fuel. The trick is to keep it simple, use ripe bananas, and wrap it well. Then you can spend less time worrying about food and more time enjoying the trail.</p>
