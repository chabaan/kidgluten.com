<p>There's something about the morning air at camp—crisp, pine-scented, and quiet. But for a child on the autism spectrum, that quiet can be filled with sensory noise: the hiss of a stove, the clatter of a pan, the unpredictable texture of food. Breakfast is often the hardest meal. That's why I've come to rely on a simple, soft scrambled egg method that feels calm on the plate and in the mouth. No crispy edges, no rubbery curds, just a gentle, custardy scramble that's as soothing to make as it is to eat.</p>

<h2>Why Texture Matters More Than Taste</h2>
<p>For many autistic eaters, texture is the first filter. A lumpy, browned, or overly firm egg can be a deal-breaker before the fork even touches the lips. Soft scrambled eggs—cooked low and slow—offer a uniform, smooth consistency that's predictable bite after bite. That predictability is comfort. It's the same reason a favorite blanket or a familiar routine helps ground a busy mind. When you're camping, where everything is new and slightly unpredictable, offering a familiar food in a consistent texture can be a small anchor.</p>

<h2>The Camp Stove Challenge</h2>
<p>Camp stoves are notoriously finicky. They blast heat in bursts, and the wind can make a low flame flicker into a high one. That's the enemy of soft eggs. The trick is to manage the heat, not just the eggs. I always set up my stove on a flat, stable surface, shielded from the wind with a small foldable windscreen (a piece of heavy-duty foil works in a pinch). Then I light it and let it warm for a full two minutes on the lowest setting before I even crack an egg. This allows the pan to heat evenly, so there are no hot spots that will seize the eggs into tough curds.</p>

<h3>Common Mistake: Turning the Heat Up to Speed Things Up</h3>
<p>I see it all the time—campers crank the flame to get breakfast done faster. With soft eggs, that's a disaster. High heat causes the proteins to contract quickly, squeezing out moisture and leaving you with dry, rubbery clumps. Instead, embrace the slow. It takes about 8 minutes for four eggs, and that's okay. Use that time to breathe in the morning, watch the light filter through the trees, and let your child see that cooking can be a calm, unhurried activity.</p>

<h2>Step-by-Step: The Gentle Method</h2>
<p>Start with a small non-stick skillet—8 inches is perfect for two to three servings. Add a teaspoon of butter or a mild oil like avocado. Let it melt on low, tilting the pan to coat the surface. Meanwhile, whisk your eggs in a bowl with a splash of milk or a dairy-free alternative. Whisk gently; you're not making a froth, just combining yolks and whites. Over-whisking incorporates air that can make the eggs puff up and then deflate, creating a spongy texture.</p>
<p>Pour the eggs into the pan and let them sit for 10 seconds. Then, using a silicone spatula, begin pushing the eggs from the edges toward the center in a slow, continuous motion. Every 15 to 20 seconds, lift the pan off the burner for about 5 seconds while still stirring. This on-and-off heat is the secret to creamy eggs. It prevents the bottom from setting too fast and allows the curds to stay small and soft. When the eggs look just set—still glossy and a little loose—pull them off the heat. They'll finish cooking in the pan's residual warmth.</p>

<h2>Adapting for Wind, Cold, and Picky Eaters</h2>
<p>Wind is your biggest opponent. Even with a windscreen, a gust can spike the flame. If you notice the eggs starting to firm up too quickly, pull the pan off the heat entirely for 30 seconds and stir. That pause rescues the texture. On cold mornings, serve the eggs immediately in a pre-warmed bowl—I pour hot water into the bowl, let it sit for a minute, then dry it out. A warm bowl keeps the eggs from cooling into a less appealing, congealed state.</p>
<p>For a child who prefers no mixed textures, serve the eggs plain, with a side of soft buttered toast cut into triangles. If they like a bit of flavor, add a tiny sprinkle of mild cheese at the very end, stirring just until it melts. Avoid strong additions like onions or peppers, which can be overwhelming. And always offer the eggs in a familiar bowl from home—the visual and tactile familiarity can make a new campsite feel more like a safe space.</p>

<h2>Safety and Cleanup Notes</h2>
<p>Always cook on a stable surface away from tent walls and sleeping bags. Keep a pot holder or a thick towel nearby to handle the hot pan. And remember, never leave a lit stove unattended. After breakfast, let the pan cool completely before washing. A quick wipe with a paper towel removes most residue; use a drop of biodegradable soap and hot water if needed. Store all food in a bear-proof container or your vehicle, especially if you're in bear country—eggs and butter are just as tempting to wildlife as they are to us.</p>

<h2>The Takeaway</h2>
<p>Soft scrambled eggs are more than a breakfast; they're a small act of care. By controlling the heat, the stirring, and the texture, you're giving your child a moment of predictability in the unpredictability of the outdoors. It's a simple dish, but it says, "I see you, and I've made this just for you." And that's a beautiful way to start a day in the woods.</p>
