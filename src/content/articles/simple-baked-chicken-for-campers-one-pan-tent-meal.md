<p>The smell of garlic and smoked paprika drifting from a covered skillet is the kind of camp dinner that makes neighboring campers wander over to ask what's cooking. Simple baked chicken doesn't need a full kitchen—just a fire, a cast iron pan (or a roll of foil), and a little patience. This method gives you tender, juicy meat with golden edges and a side of vegetables all in one pan. Cleanup is a breeze, which means more time around the fire.</p>

<h2>Why Baked Chicken Works at Camp</h2>
<p>Baking—or more accurately, dry-heat cooking in a covered vessel—is forgiving. Unlike grilling, where flare-ups and burnt edges are common, a covered skillet traps steam and heat evenly. That means you don't have to babysit the chicken. It's the perfect hands-off meal after a long day of hiking.</p>
<p>The key is to use bone-in, skin-on thighs. They stay moist even if you get distracted by the sunset. Chicken breasts dry out quickly, especially over a campfire where temperature control is guesswork.</p>

<h2>What You Need</h2>
<p>You don't need a full camp kitchen. Here's the short list:</p>
<ul>
<li>A 10-inch cast iron skillet with a lid (or heavy-duty foil)</li>
<li>Long-handled tongs and a spatula</li>
<li>Olive oil or any cooking oil</li>
<li>Salt, pepper, and your favorite seasoning (smoked paprika adds a nice campfire touch)</li>
<li>Chicken thighs (bone-in, skin-on)</li>
<li>Vegetables that hold up: onions, carrots, celery, potatoes</li>
<li>A lemon and a few sprigs of thyme (optional but worth it)</li>
<li>Chicken broth or water</li>
</ul>

<h2>The Method: Cast Iron Skillet</h2>
<h3>Step 1: Get Your Fire Ready</h3>
<p>Don't start cooking over roaring flames. Let your fire burn down to hot coals—that's the sweet spot. You want a steady, medium heat that won't scorch the bottom of the pan. If you're using a camp stove, set it to medium-low.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Sear the Chicken</h3>
<p>Pat the chicken dry. This is non-negotiable—wet skin won't brown. Rub with oil and season generously. Heat the skillet for a few minutes, then add a splash of oil. Place the chicken skin-side down. Let it sizzle for 3-4 minutes without moving it. You want a golden crust, not a burnt one. Flip and sear the other side for 2 minutes.</p>
<p><strong>Common mistake:</strong> crowding the pan. If the chicken pieces touch, they steam instead of sear. Cook in batches if needed.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Add the Vegetables and Liquid</h3>
<p>Scatter the onion, garlic, carrots, and celery around the chicken. Lay lemon slices on top. Pour in the broth—about half a cup—carefully, avoiding the skin. This creates steam that bakes the chicken evenly. Cover with a lid or foil.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Bake, Don't Peek</h3>
<p>Resist the urge to lift the lid. Every time you do, you lose heat and add minutes. Let it cook undisturbed for 25-30 minutes. Then check: the chicken should register 165°F in the thickest part, and the vegetables should be fork-tender. If the liquid evaporates, add a splash more broth.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Rest and Serve</h3>
<p>Let the chicken rest for 5 minutes off the heat. This lets the juices redistribute. Serve straight from the skillet—it keeps everything warm.</p>

<h2>The Foil Packet Alternative</h2>
<p>No skillet? No problem. Foil packets are the ultimate camp hack. They're lightweight, require zero cleanup, and cook perfectly over coals.</p>
<p>Place each chicken thigh on a large sheet of heavy-duty foil. Add your vegetables, a spoonful of broth, a lemon slice, and a sprig of thyme. Season well. Fold the foil into a tight packet, leaving a little room for steam. Cook over hot coals for 25-30 minutes, flipping once halfway. The steam cooks the chicken gently, and the juices create a light sauce.</p>
<p><strong>Pro tip:</strong> double-wrap if you're using regular foil. A torn packet means a lost dinner.</p>

<h2>Adapting for Wind, Rain, and Cold</h2>
<p>Wind is the enemy of even heat. If you're cooking on a camp stove, set up a windbreak—a large rock or a cooler works. For a campfire, position your skillet so the wind blows the flames away from the pan. In rain, move under a tarp or canopy. The skillet method works fine in light drizzle, but foil packets are easier to manage if you're crouched under a tarp.</p>
<p>In cold weather, your fire will need more fuel to maintain heat. Start with a larger fire and let it burn down to a bigger bed of coals. You may need to add a few minutes to the cooking time.</p>

<h2>Safety Notes</h2>
<p>Always cook chicken to 165°F. Use a food thermometer—they're small and cheap, and they save you from guessing.</p>
<p>Never leave a lit stove or fire unattended. Keep a bucket of water nearby, and fully extinguish the fire before heading to your tent.</p>
<p>If you're cooking in bear country, store all food scraps in a bear-proof container or hang them away from your sleeping area. Never bring food into your tent—the smell lingers and attracts wildlife.</p>

<h2>The Takeaway</h2>
<p>Simple baked chicken is a camp dinner that feels like a luxury. With a little prep and the right technique, you can have a hot, satisfying meal that doesn't demand hours of cleanup. Whether you use a cast iron skillet or foil packets, the secret is low, steady heat and letting the chicken cook undisturbed. Try it on your next trip—your campmates will thank you.</p>
